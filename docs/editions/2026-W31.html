The Week in Littleton · Jul 27 – Aug 2, 2026

A New School Takes Shape While Littleton Tends Its Roots

*A landmark week for Littleton's future finds the town investing in its children, its land, and its water all at once.*

Compiled by Littleton Signal across 7 sources

After years of planning and a building that has served Littleton's youngest learners for six decades, the path to a new Shaker Lane Elementary School cleared a critical hurdle this week when the Massachusetts School Building Authority approved the project's budget. The Shaker Lane School Building Committee had gathered just days earlier for its July 22nd meeting, and the MSBA's green light means the town can now move in earnest toward replacing a 64-year-old structure that has long outlived its useful life. The timing feels significant: it arrives in the same week that Littleton Middle School earned a nomination for a National Blue Ribbon award and the high school received state recognition — a reminder that the community's investment in public education is already paying dividends, even as the physical infrastructure struggles to keep pace.

Schools on the Rise

The school news this week arrived in two distinct registers — celebration and construction — but both point in the same direction. Littleton Middle School's Blue Ribbon nomination and the high school's state recognition place Littleton Public Schools among Massachusetts's acknowledged high performers, a distinction the state has now formalized. Meanwhile, the MSBA budget approval for Shaker Lane moves the elementary school project past one of its most consequential early milestones. The School Committee's Negotiating Subcommittee also met on July 24th, signaling that the organizational work running alongside the building project continues at a steady pace. For a town of Littleton's size, having all three school levels in the news at once — and for good reasons — is no small thing.

A 64-year-old school, a $20.25 million water lawsuit, and a trail named for a three-decade volunteer — Littleton's past and future are in conversation this week.

Water and the Long Fight

Littleton's relationship with its water supply has been complicated and costly for years, and two items this week illustrated both the burden and the resolve. The Littleton Water Department has filed a federal lawsuit seeking to recover the $20.25 million cost of constructing a treatment plant to remove PFAS contamination from the town's drinking water — a legal move that puts the financial fight squarely in the courts. At the same time, a Level 3 Critical Drought declaration has triggered a ban on all nonessential outdoor water use, including lawn irrigation and car washing. Residents already contending with water quality concerns are now being asked to conserve what's there. The town's new $45 million common sewer system, which came in ahead of schedule, offers a note of infrastructure optimism amid the drought stress — but the ribbon cutting on that project also underscores just how much Littleton has been spending to keep its essential systems sound.

Land and Legacy

On a warmer note, the Littleton Conservation Trust officially opened the Art Lazarus Trail at Long Lake Park Conservation Area this week, naming it for a volunteer who gave 33 years of service to Littleton's conservation effort. Trail openings don't often make front pages, but this one carries weight: it is a direct expression of the volunteer-driven, incremental work that keeps green space accessible in a town under development pressure. The Conservation Commission met on July 21st, the Community Preservation Committee on July 15th, and an LCTV episode recently featured the Conservation Trust's president discussing volunteer opportunities — all of it part of the ongoing civic metabolism that produces trails like this one. The Littleton Trails Hiking Challenge remains open to new participants, and the Reuben Hoar Library will host a talk on the dangers of yard pesticides to insects and wildlife, keeping the conservation conversation active beyond committee rooms.

Ballots and Boards

With the September 1st state primary approaching, the Town Clerk's office is reminding residents that in-person early voting is now available. The Select Board meets Monday, July 27th, continuing a regular summer cadence that has included joint sessions with the Finance Committee and a board retreat — the kind of behind-the-scenes governance work that rarely generates headlines but keeps the town running. The Planning Board, Zoning Board of Appeals, and Affordable Housing Trust have all met in recent weeks as well, suggesting that land-use questions continue to move through the pipeline. Property owners also have a near-term deadline to note: real estate taxes are due by August 3rd.
