The Week in Littleton · August 3–9, 2026

Water Woes, New Trails, and a Town Counting Its Costs

*A week in which Littleton's long battles with contamination, drought, and infrastructure come into sharp focus — even as a new trail invites residents to simply walk outside.*

Compiled by Littleton Signal across 7 sources

If there is a single thread running through Littleton this week, it is water — its scarcity, its safety, and its price. Real estate taxes came due on August 3, the same day the Select Board and Finance Committee sat down together at Town Hall, and residents paying those bills might reasonably wonder what their money has been buying. The answer, it turns out, is a great deal: a $45 million sewer system that ribbon-cut into full operation after coming in on schedule last fall, a $20.25 million PFAS treatment plant at Spectacle Pond now the subject of a federal lawsuit to recover those costs, and a $285,522 state grant to replace aging electrical reclosers across the distribution grid. Littleton has been spending — and, in some cases, fighting — to keep the lights on and the water clean.

The Water Bill

The Littleton Water Department's federal lawsuit to recoup the $20.25 million it spent building a PFAS treatment plant at Spectacle Pond is the kind of story that takes years to unfold but deserves a close read now. That plant exists because the town's drinking water was contaminated; the lawsuit is the town's attempt to make someone else bear the cost. Meanwhile, a Level 3 Critical Drought declared by MassDEP has prompted a ban on all nonessential outdoor water use — no lawn irrigation, no car washing, no hosing down driveways. Residents checking their 2025 water quality report, now posted online by the department, will find a utility that monitors its aquifers and watersheds carefully. The drought is a reminder that careful monitoring is not the same as having enough water.

Governing in August

The week opened with the Select Board and Finance Committee meeting jointly on Monday evening in Room 103 — a pairing that typically signals budget or policy matters that need both bodies in the same room. The Planning Board gathered Thursday at the Center on Shattuck Street, where road work on the traffic island has been causing delays since mid-July. A Conservation Commission hearing on 35 Shaker Lane is set for August 18, and the Shaker Lane School Building Committee held its most recent session in late July — a reminder that the school construction conversation continues quietly in the background even as summer winds down. The Zoning Board of Appeals, which published minutes from its June hearing on 6 Long Lake Road, meets again on August 20.

Long Lake, Two Ways

Long Lake offered a small study in contrasts this week. On Friday, August 7, the lake closes entirely to boating and swimming so crews can treat invasive aquatic plants — an intervention that speaks to how much maintenance wild places quietly require. And yet just steps away, the Littleton Conservation Trust recently opened the Art Lazarus Trail at Long Lake Park Conservation Area, honoring a volunteer who gave 33 years to local conservation. The Littleton Trails Hiking Challenge is still running and open to new participants. The message from the natural side of town is consistent: these places are worth protecting, and the work is never quite finished.

On the Grid

Littleton Electric Light and Water Departments has had an eventful stretch. The utility's $285,522 state grant to replace six aging reclosers will mean shorter, more contained outages when the grid is stressed. Less comfortably, LELWD's 2025 disclosure of foreign hackers targeting its computer systems was recently featured on '60 Minutes,' bringing national attention to a cybersecurity incident the utility first went public with in March of last year. There is also a FLOCK camera program update in circulation, though details remain sparse. Taken together, the picture is of a municipal utility that is both investing in resilience and navigating scrutiny it did not entirely choose.

Schools and Community

The School Committee's Negotiating Subcommittee met in late July at Reuben Hoar Library, and the CLUB Afterschool program is asking families to visit its new website and weigh in on how the program can improve. A free public talk called 'Silent Spring II,' scheduled at Reuben Hoar Library, will take up the damage done by yard pesticides — including an 80 to 90 percent drop in Monarch butterfly populations. It is a fitting companion to the Art Lazarus Trail opening: both ask residents to think about what kind of place Littleton wants to be, not just for people, but for everything living here.
