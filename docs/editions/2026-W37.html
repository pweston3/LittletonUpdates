The Week in Littleton · September 7–13, 2026

Schools Shine, Water Strains, and a Trail Opens Its Gates

*A week of hard-won civic milestones — from a 64-year-old school finally getting its replacement to a drought that's testing the town's patience.*

Compiled by Littleton Signal across 7 sources

It has been a week in Littleton that rewards paying attention. The Massachusetts School Building Authority formally approved the budget for a replacement Shaker Lane Elementary School, putting state muscle behind a project years in the making to retire a 64-year-old building. At the same time, the schools inside that aging district are earning outsized praise: Littleton Middle School has been nominated for a National Blue Ribbon award, ranked among the top-performing schools in the state, and Littleton High School has landed in the top ten public high schools in Greater Boston. There is something quietly moving about a town fighting to build a better school for kids who are already thriving in the one they have.

The School Question

The Shaker Lane School Building Committee has been meeting steadily through the summer — virtually in May, June, July, and August — and the pace of that work now has a formal foundation beneath it: the MSBA's budget approval means the project is no longer aspirational. It is a plan. The Select Board and Finance Committee are scheduled to meet jointly on Monday, September 14, and the school replacement project's financial implications will almost certainly be part of the conversation in Room 103. Residents who want to understand what comes next should make time for that meeting. The academic recognition arriving alongside this capital investment is a useful reminder of what the community is protecting. The middle school's Blue Ribbon nomination and the high school's Greater Boston ranking are not just ribbons on a wall — they reflect a district performing at a level that makes the argument for investment easy to make.

Water Under Pressure

Littleton's relationship with its water is complicated this week. On one hand, the town is operating under a Level 3 Critical Drought designation, the kind that bans all nonessential outdoor water use — lawn irrigation, car washing, surface cleaning. Residents should treat their hoses as idle for the foreseeable future. On the other hand, the Littleton Water Department published its 2025 annual water quality report, a reminder that behind the faucet is a serious operation: 150 monitoring wells, active aquifer zoning, and a comprehensive watershed management program. The department is also fighting on a third front. It has filed a federal lawsuit to recover the $20.25 million it spent building a treatment plant to remove PFAS contamination from Spectacle Pond drinking water. That figure — the cost of making the water safe — sits uncomfortably alongside the drought restrictions, painting a picture of a water system under real and compounding pressure. Separately, the $45 million Littleton Common Sewer System, which finished construction on schedule in fall 2025, is now fully operational, replacing septic systems and positioning the town center for economic development.

Land and Legacy

The week carried a warmer note too. Littleton officially opened the Art Lazarus Trail at Long Lake Park Conservation Area, honoring a volunteer who gave 33 years to local conservation. A ribbon-cutting ceremony marked the occasion — the kind of community moment that doesn't make national news but means everything to the people who knew him and to those who will walk that trail without knowing his name. The Littleton Conservation Trust's hiking challenge continues, inviting residents to log their own miles on local trails, and an upcoming talk at Reuben Hoar Library will take up the dangers of yard pesticides to insects and ecosystems. The Conservation Commission met on September 8 and has kept a steady rhythm of meetings through the summer.

Governance in Motion

Beyond Monday's joint Select Board and Finance Committee meeting, the Charter Committee convenes September 21 virtually — worth watching for anyone interested in how Littleton's foundational governance documents might evolve. The Zoning Board of Appeals meets September 17, and the Planning Board has been active, with a September 3 meeting already in the books. Minutes from a string of summer meetings — Select Board sessions with the Conservation Commission, the Board of Assessors, and the Park and Recreation Commission — have all been posted, giving residents a chance to catch up on a summer of quieter but consequential local work.
