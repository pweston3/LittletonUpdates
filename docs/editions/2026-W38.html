The Week in Littleton · September 14–20, 2026

A New School, a New Sewer, and a Town Building Its Future

*Littleton is spending this September making big bets on infrastructure — for its children, its downtown, and its water.*

Compiled by Littleton Signal across 8 sources

It has been a week of momentum in Littleton, the kind where years of planning suddenly snap into focus. The Massachusetts School Building Authority formally approved the budget for a new Shaker Lane School, moving the town a critical step closer to replacing a 64-year-old building that has long outlived its useful life. At the same time, the ribbon was cut on the new $45 million Common Sewer System — a nearly two-year construction project already drawing economic activity to downtown. And beneath all of it, a quieter but urgent story: the town's water department has filed a federal lawsuit to recover the $20.25 million it spent building a treatment plant to clean PFAS contamination from Spectacle Pond, the drinking water supply that Littleton families depend on. Big projects, big bills, and a town working hard to make sure someone else pays part of them.

School on the Move

The Shaker Lane School project cleared a major hurdle this week when the Massachusetts School Building Authority approved its construction budget, giving the town the green light it needed to move forward in earnest. The Building Committee met virtually on September 16th, and the School Committee's budget and policy subcommittees were also active through the week — a signal that behind the headlines, the unglamorous work of planning a new school is well underway. The existing building is 64 years old. Replacing it has been a long time coming, and this week's MSBA approval means the conversation is no longer about whether to build, but how.

Big projects, big bills — and a town working to make sure someone else pays part of them.

Sewer and Water

Downtown Littleton marked a milestone with the ribbon cutting on its new Common Sewer System. The project, which took nearly two years to complete and cost $45 million, drew $13 million in state and federal grants and is already showing signs of spurring economic activity in the town center. It is the kind of infrastructure investment that rarely gets celebrated the way it should — until you consider what it unlocks. Meanwhile, Littleton Electric Light and Water received a $285,522 state grant to replace six aging reclosers on power lines serving Littleton and Boxborough, a targeted fix that will help shorten and contain outages when they occur.

The PFAS Fight

Less celebratory is the situation at Spectacle Pond. The Littleton Water Department has filed a federal lawsuit seeking to recover the $20.25 million cost of a treatment plant built to remove PFAS contamination from the town's drinking water supply. The lawsuit puts Littleton among the growing number of municipalities across the country pushing back on the manufacturers and distributors responsible for PFAS pollution — insisting that communities should not be left holding the bill for a contamination crisis they did not create. The outcome will be worth watching.

Drought and the Land

Residents should know: Littleton is currently under a Level 3 Critical Drought, and all nonessential outdoor water use — lawn irrigation, car washing, exterior surface cleaning — is banned. This is not a suggestion. Against that backdrop, the opening of the Art Lazarus Trail at Long Lake Park Conservation Area carries particular resonance. The trail is named for a volunteer who gave 33 years to conservation work in Littleton, and its official opening by the Littleton Conservation Trust is a reminder of what sustained community stewardship looks like. For those looking to get outside, the town's trails hiking challenge is also ongoing. A free talk at the Reuben Hoar Library on the environmental and health risks of yard pesticides rounds out a week with conservation quietly at its center.

Governing the Week Ahead

The coming days are dense with public meetings. The Finance Committee will sit down jointly with the Affordable Housing Trust on September 22nd — a pairing that signals affordable housing is moving up the fiscal agenda. The Conservation Commission meets the same evening, and the Charter Committee convenes September 21st. The Select Board has meetings on both September 23rd and September 28th. The Town Clerk's office has also released early information about the November 2026 local election, so residents should start paying attention to what and who will be on the ballot.
