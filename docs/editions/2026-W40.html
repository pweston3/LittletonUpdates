The Week in Littleton · Sep 28 – Oct 4, 2026

Schools Shine, Cameras Caught, and a Town Builds Forward

*A week in which Littleton's ambitions for its children, its water, and its open land ran headlong into harder questions about trust and accountability.*

Compiled by Littleton Signal across 8 sources

The biggest story in Littleton this week isn't a single event — it's a pattern. The Massachusetts School Building Authority approved the budget for the new Shaker Lane School, capping a milestone year since voters said yes to replacing a 64-year-old building that has outlived its time. Meanwhile, Littleton Middle School earned a nomination for a National Blue Ribbon award and was named one of the top-performing schools in the state, and the high school collected a state honor of its own. Five students were recognized for excellence in world languages. By almost any measure, this is a school system that is working. And yet the week also brought a deeply troubling reminder that institutions must be watched: a Marlborough man who drove a school bus in Littleton has been charged with raping a child, a charge that will rightly demand answers from anyone responsible for student safety.

Building a School

With the MSBA's budget approval in hand, the Shaker Lane School project moves from promise to plan. Town officials — the superintendent, town administrator, and project architect — will hold a public Q&A on October 14 at the Center on Shattuck to mark one year since the vote. That meeting is worth attending: it is the moment when a community gets to see exactly what it said yes to, and to ask the questions that come only once construction is real. The building committee has been meeting regularly, most recently in September, and the Planning Board's October 8 agenda includes site-plan hearings at 550 King Street and 511 Newtown Road — a reminder that Littleton's growth is being shaped on multiple fronts at once.

Cameras and Trust

The most unsettling story of the week has nothing to do with schools or construction. After the Select Board ordered all six Flock Safety license-plate cameras shut down in May, five of the six were found to have been secretly reactivated by the vendor without notifying the town. The Littleton Police Department has issued a public update, and the Select Board meets October 5 — expect this to be at the center of that agenda. Littleton is not alone: four other communities ended their Flock contracts within five days of the news. The episode raises a question that goes beyond any single vendor: when a town says stop, who is responsible for making sure it actually stops?

Water and Land

Littleton's relationship with its natural resources has been expensive, litigious, and — in some corners — quietly celebratory all in the same week. The Water Department has filed a federal lawsuit to recover costs from a $20.25 million treatment plant built to scrub PFAS contamination from the Spectacle Pond drinking water supply. Separately, the new $45 million Littleton Common Sewer System is fully operational, completed on schedule and backed by $13 million in state and federal grants — a genuine infrastructure win. And the electric and water departments received a $285,522 state grant to replace aging equipment that reroutes power during outages. Underneath all of it, a Level 1 Mild Drought has the town restricting nonessential outdoor watering to one day per week.

What's Coming

Early in-person voting for the state election opens October 17 from 9 a.m. to 3 p.m. at 37 Shattuck Street. The Zoning Board of Appeals convenes October 15 for hearings on three properties — 3 Nicole Lane, 2 Cataldo Pond Way, and 200 Whitcomb Avenue — and the Conservation Commission takes up 511 Newtown Road on October 20. The calendar is full, and the questions are real.
