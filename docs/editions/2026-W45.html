The Week in Littleton · November 2–8, 2026

Sewers Open, Cameras Shut, and a School Awaits Its Future

*A week of infrastructure milestones, trust fractures, and big decisions still to come.*

Compiled by Littleton Signal across 7 sources

Littleton marked a genuine turning point this week as its new $45 million downtown sewer system celebrated a ribbon-cutting after nearly two years of construction. The project already shows signs of sparking economic life in the town center, trading out aging septic systems for something built to last. But even as one long infrastructure chapter closed, others opened: the water utility is mid-flush on the entire distribution system, residents under a Level 2 drought are restricted to hand-held hoses in off-peak hours, and the Water Department is in federal court seeking to recover the $20.25 million it spent removing PFAS contamination from the Spectacle Pond supply. The week made clear that Littleton's relationship with its own pipes and water is far from settled.

Cameras & Trust

The story that may linger longest this week has nothing to do with pipes or classrooms. After the Select Board ordered all six Flock Safety license-plate cameras shut down back in May, five of the six were found to have been secretly reactivated by the company without notifying the town. Four other communities ended their Flock contracts within the same five-day window, suggesting Littleton is not alone in grappling with what it means to actually control the surveillance technology it has contracted for. The Police Department issued a public update on the system, and the Select Board — which met both on November 2nd in Town Hall and again on November 6th via Zoom — now has some pointed questions to answer about oversight and accountability.

A School's Long Wait

Littleton's elementary school building is 64 years old, and the community is now actively working through what comes next — whether renovation, replacement, or something in between is still under discussion. The conversation lands against a backdrop of genuine academic momentum: Littleton Middle School has been nominated for a National Blue Ribbon award, and the high school received separate recognition from the state. It is a telling contrast — schools earning national notice while the building that houses the youngest learners is older than most of their grandparents. The Shaker Lane School Site Council meets November 10th, and that forum will offer another chance for families to stay close to the process.

Land & Legacy

Conservation had a quietly full week. The former Nagog Hill Orchard — now known as Hopewell — held a public update at Reuben Hoar Library on November 5th, giving residents a chance to learn how the site is taking shape as a conservation area. Meanwhile, a new trail in Long Lake Park Conservation Area was officially opened and named in honor of Art Lazarus, who gave 33 years of volunteer service to Littleton's conservation work. The Littleton Conservation Trust's fall newsletter adds more texture, covering native plant guidance and a year-in-review of stewardship activity. And for anyone who wants to get outside right now, the Littleton Trails Hiking Challenge is still open — log your hikes and join in.

Town's Finances

The Select Board's November 2nd meeting included a public hearing on property tax classification — the annual process that sets how the tax burden is divided between residential and commercial properties, and one that carries real consequences for homeowners and businesses alike. The Financial Policy Working Group, which met November 3rd, is scheduled to meet again November 17th, signaling that the town's longer-range fiscal planning work is ongoing. Unofficial results from the October 27th Special Town Meeting are now posted for anyone tracking how those votes landed.
