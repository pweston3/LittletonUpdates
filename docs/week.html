The Week in Littleton · August 10–16, 2026

Big Wins, Broken Trust, and a Town Counting Its Water

*A week of ribbon-cuttings and recognition was shadowed by a surveillance betrayal and a drought that isn't letting go.*

Compiled by Littleton Signal across 7 sources

For a town of about 10,000 people, Littleton packed a remarkable amount of news into a single week. The Massachusetts School Building Authority signed off on the budget to replace the 64-year-old Shaker Lane Elementary School, the new $45 million Common Sewer System celebrated its ribbon-cutting after nearly two years of construction, and the town's middle and high schools earned state and national recognition for academic excellence. It was, by any measure, a week of institutional momentum. But running beneath all of it — like the contaminated groundwater Littleton has spent years cleaning up — was a quieter, more unsettling story about who controls the town's public spaces, and who can be trusted to respect that control.

School Days Ahead

The approval of the Shaker Lane Elementary School replacement budget by the MSBA is the kind of milestone that takes years to reach and feels anticlimactic when it arrives — until you remember that the building it will replace has been standing for 64 years. The Building Committee meets virtually on August 19th, and a Planning Board public hearing on the school's stormwater management and site plan is set for September 3rd, meaning the project is now moving from concept into the concrete business of permits and neighbors' questions. It is worth paying attention to both meetings. Meanwhile, the schools that students are currently attending earned some notable praise. Littleton Middle School was nominated for a National Blue Ribbon award and named one of the top-performing schools in the state; Littleton High School received its own state-level recognition. These are not participation trophies — they reflect real instructional work happening in buildings that residents pass every day.

Infrastructure Moment

Two major utility projects reached visible milestones this week, and both are worth celebrating — carefully. The $45 million Common Sewer System is now fully operational, and officials say it is already encouraging economic development and improving groundwater quality in the town center. That is a genuine achievement after nearly two years of construction. Separately, Littleton Electric Light and Water Departments were awarded a $285,522 state grant to install updated reclosers that will reduce power outages for residents and neighboring Boxborough. But the Water Department's week was not entirely celebratory. It filed a federal lawsuit seeking to recover the $20.25 million it spent building a treatment plant to remove PFAS contamination from the town's drinking water supply — a reminder that the costs of industrial pollution land, first and hardest, on the communities that did not cause them. And with Littleton now under a Level 3 Critical Drought, all nonessential outdoor water use is banned. The lawn can wait.

The Camera Problem

The most troubling story of the week involves Flock Safety, the license plate reader company whose cameras the Select Board previously ordered shut down. Five of the six cameras that Littleton had deactivated were quietly reactivated by the company — without notifying the town. Littleton is not alone: four other communities ended their Flock contracts within the same five-day window. The Littleton Police Department has issued a public update on the situation, and the Select Board and Conservation Commission held a joint meeting on August 10th, though what was discussed there is not yet fully public. This is not a minor administrative dispute. A private vendor secretly turning cameras back on after a town's elected board ordered them off is a direct challenge to local governance. Residents deserve a full accounting of how this happened, what data was collected during the unauthorized reactivation, and what the town's contractual options are. Watch this one closely.

Trails and Trust

Not everything this week ran on conflict. Littleton officially opened the Art Lazarus Trail at Long Lake Park Conservation Area, honoring a volunteer who gave 33 years to local conservation. The Littleton Conservation Trust is holding a ribbon-cutting ceremony, and the town's ongoing hiking challenge continues to draw residents onto its network of conservation paths. The Conservation Trust's president, Dustin Neild, appeared on a recent LCTV episode to discuss the organization's work and volunteer opportunities — a good entry point for anyone who wants to get involved. A free talk at the Reuben Hoar Library on the environmental and health dangers of yard pesticides rounds out a week in which Littleton's conservation community was, as usual, doing quiet, durable work. The Level 3 drought is a reminder of why that work matters.
