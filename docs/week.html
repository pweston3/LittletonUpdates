The Week in Littleton · November 16–22, 2026

A New School, a New Sewer, and a Town Building Its Future

*Littleton enters the week with big-ticket projects moving from planning to reality — and a conservation community quietly doing the same.*

Compiled by Littleton Signal across 8 sources

Some weeks in a small town are mostly calendar — meetings scheduled, agendas posted, minutes filed. This is not one of those weeks. The Massachusetts School Building Authority has formally approved the budget for a new Shaker Lane Elementary School, clearing one of the highest hurdles in a years-long effort to replace a 64-year-old building that has long outlived its design life. At the same time, Littleton's $45 million downtown sewer system reached a ribbon-cutting milestone after two years of construction, retiring septic systems at the town's commercial core and opening the door to economic development that simply wasn't possible before. Two major public investments, moving in the same week from promise to reality — that's the throughline.

School Built Here

The MSBA budget approval for Shaker Lane is the kind of news that takes years to arrive and then lands all at once. The building the approval is meant to replace has been educating Littleton children since the early 1960s, and the case for replacement has grown harder to dismiss with each passing decade. With the state authority now on board, the Shaker Lane School Building Committee convened virtually on November 18th to work through what comes next — a process that will involve the community at every step. The School Committee meets the following evening, and subcommittees on policy and research had already gathered Monday, signaling that the district is in an active, coordinated push heading into the holiday stretch. The school news doesn't stop at construction. Littleton Public Schools received state recognition for educational excellence this week, and Littleton Middle School earned a nomination for a National Blue Ribbon award while the high school collected its own separate state honor. It is, in short, a good moment to be building a new school in a district that keeps earning the right to be proud of itself.

Downtown, Connected

The ribbon cutting on Littleton's new downtown sewer system is the kind of civic accomplishment that's easy to underestimate until you remember what it replaces: a patchwork of aging septic systems that quietly constrained what the town center could become. The $45 million project took two years to build and now makes the downtown a fundamentally different place for anyone thinking about investment, renovation, or new development. The Planning Board meets Thursday, November 19th, and the Affordable Housing Trust convened Tuesday — both bodies now operating in a town center with infrastructure that wasn't there last year.

Water on Two Fronts

Littleton's water story this week runs in two directions at once. The Littleton Water Department has filed a federal lawsuit seeking to recover the $20.25 million it spent constructing a treatment plant to remove PFAS contamination from a town drinking water well — a fight to hold polluters accountable for costs that fell on ratepayers. Meanwhile, LELWD is working through its zone-by-zone fall flushing of the water distribution system, reminding residents that temporary discolored tap water is a planned part of maintenance, not a cause for alarm. The same utility earned a national gold-level customer satisfaction award from the American Public Power Association this week, scoring above 95 percent — a reminder that the people managing Littleton's pipes and wires are doing something right even as they fight in federal court to be made whole.

Land and Legacy

The Littleton Conservation Trust has had a quietly remarkable season. This week brings the official opening of the Art Lazarus Trail at Long Lake Park Conservation Area, named in honor of a volunteer who gave 33 years to conservation work in town — the kind of tribute that means something precisely because it's earned. The LCT's Fall 2026 newsletter is out, covering native plant guidance and recent projects, and the organization's hiking trail challenge remains open for new participants. Separately, the former Nagog Hill Orchard — now known as Hopewell — was the subject of a recent public meeting at the library, as residents weigh in on the future of that conservation land. The Conservation Commission has a full calendar ahead, with public hearings scheduled on properties along Tahattawan Road, Great Road, Green Needles Road, and Spruce Street running into December. Taken together, it's a week that illustrates something Littleton does well: tend its open land even as it builds.

At Town Hall

The Select Board, which meets Monday evening at Town Hall, issued a formal statement this week regarding the Town Administrator's employment contract — a development worth watching as details become clearer. The Financial Policy Working Group also gathered Tuesday, and the Taxation Aid Committee meets Thursday morning. The unofficial results of the October 27th Special Town Meeting have been released, closing the loop on that session's business. MassDOT has announced a virtual public hearing on plans to replace the Route 119 bridge deck over I-495, adding a state-level infrastructure question to a week already dense with local ones.
