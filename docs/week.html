The Week in Littleton · Jul 27 – Aug 2, 2026

A New School, a New Sewer, and a Town Running Dry

*Littleton enters August with generational infrastructure projects in motion — and a drought that's putting everyday life on pause.*

Compiled by Littleton Signal across 8 sources

It was a week that underscored just how much is being built, debated, and strained in Littleton all at once. The Massachusetts School Building Authority approved the budget for a replacement Shaker Lane Elementary School — a milestone for a building that has stood for 64 years and long outlived its useful life. At the same time, the town cut the ribbon on a brand-new common sewer system, the product of nearly two years of construction and a $45 million investment that officials say will reshape the town center's economic future. And threading through all of it: a Level 3 Critical Drought that has banned all nonessential outdoor water use, a vivid reminder that even a town investing in its future has to reckon with the limits of its land.

School of the Future

The Shaker Lane Elementary project cleared a major hurdle this week when the Massachusetts School Building Authority signed off on the project budget, giving the town the state partnership it needs to move forward on replacing a 64-year-old building. The Shaker Lane School Building Committee met on July 22nd, and the School Committee's negotiating subcommittee convened days later — signs that the institutional machinery is now in motion. The project will require sustained community engagement in the months ahead, and a Conservation Commission public hearing on the Shaker Lane property is already on the calendar for August 18th. The school news doesn't stop there. Littleton Middle School has been nominated for a National Blue Ribbon award, and the high school separately received a state-level commendation — a moment worth savoring as the district simultaneously grapples with the long work of rebuilding its elementary facilities.

A 64-year-old school, a brand-new sewer, a federal PFAS lawsuit, and a drought: Littleton is building its future while managing the costs of its past.

Infrastructure Milestone

After nearly two years of digging, the town's new Common Sewer System is fully operational. The $45 million project replaces septic systems in the town center — a change that removes a longstanding constraint on development and sets the stage for economic growth along Littleton's main corridors. The ribbon cutting marks an endpoint for construction disruption and, town officials hope, a starting line for what comes next. The Select Board and Finance Committee are meeting jointly on Monday, August 3rd, which may offer an early glimpse at how leadership is thinking about the fiscal landscape those investments have created.

Water Under Pressure

Littleton's relationship with water is complicated right now. On one front, the Littleton Water Department has filed a federal lawsuit seeking to recover the $20.25 million it spent building a treatment plant to remove PFAS contamination from the Spectacle Pond drinking water supply — a fight to hold polluters accountable for costs that fell on local ratepayers. On another front, the town is deep in a Level 3 Critical Drought, with all nonessential outdoor water use prohibited. Lawn irrigation, car washing, and surface cleaning are all off the table for now. Residents with real estate tax bills due August 3rd have that deadline on their minds too, making for a week where the water department's challenges felt close to home in more ways than one.

Land and Legacy

Away from the meeting rooms, Littleton marked a quieter but meaningful moment: the formal dedication of the Art Lazarus Trail at Long Lake Park Conservation Area, honoring a volunteer who gave 33 years of service to local conservation. The Littleton Conservation Trust's ceremony put a name and a story to the miles of protected land that residents hike and paddle past without always knowing their history. It also sets a fitting backdrop for an upcoming free talk at Reuben Hoar Library on yard pesticides and the steep decline in monarch butterfly populations — a reminder that conservation isn't only about land acquisition, but about the choices made on every lawn in town.

Boards in Motion

The coming days are dense with civic activity. The Select Board and Finance Committee hold their joint session Monday evening; the Conservation Commission, Board of Assessors, and Historical Commission all meet Tuesday; and the Planning Board convenes Thursday. For residents tracking any of these threads — the school project, sewer financing, land use near conservation areas — the next two weeks offer ample opportunity to watch, and to weigh in.
