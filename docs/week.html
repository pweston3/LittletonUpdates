The Week in Littleton · Aug 31 – Sep 6, 2026

New Sewer, Old School, and a Water Fight Littleton Can't Ignore

*A week of big infrastructure milestones and bigger questions about who pays for the town Littleton is becoming.*

Compiled by Littleton Signal across 6 sources

After two years of construction and $45 million in investment, Littleton's new Common Sewer System is fully operational — a ribbon-cutting moment that marks a genuine turning point for the town center. The timing is notable: Littleton is simultaneously wrestling with a 64-year-old elementary school that the state has now agreed needs replacing, a federal lawsuit over drinking-water contamination that cost $20.25 million to remediate, and a Level 3 Critical Drought that has banned all nonessential outdoor watering. The throughline is impossible to miss. Littleton is a town investing heavily in its own future — and working hard to make sure someone else picks up the tab for the damage done to it.

Town Center Transformed

The ribbon cutting on the Common Sewer System is the kind of milestone that sounds bureaucratic until you consider what it actually means: businesses and homes in the town center that have long operated on aging septic systems are now connected to modern infrastructure, and economic development is already being cited as a result. It's the sort of unglamorous civic investment that makes everything else possible — new housing, new commercial activity, a more resilient downtown. The Select Board, which met on August 31st alongside the Board of Assessors and the Park and Recreation Commission, has plenty on its plate as the town absorbs what this new capacity makes possible.

Littleton didn't create the contamination — it simply had to pay to fix it.

School on the Move

The other major capital story of the moment is Shaker Lane Elementary School, a 64-year-old building that Littleton is now formally moving to replace. The Massachusetts School Building Authority approved the project budget — a significant green light that clears the path toward construction. The Shaker Lane School Building Committee has been meeting virtually every month since at least May, with minutes and recordings posted publicly, and the pace suggests a project that is gaining momentum rather than stalling. A School Committee meeting is set for September 10th, where the school year's opening business will likely include an update on where the project stands. Against that backdrop, the news from the schools this week carried real emotional weight. Littleton Middle School earned a nomination for a national Blue Ribbon award, and the high school received separate state-level recognition — reminders that even as the buildings age, the people inside them are doing something right. Those honors arrived alongside the sobering news that a former school bus aide pleaded guilty to assaulting nonverbal autistic children in 2024, a case that has cast a long shadow over the district.

Water Under Pressure

Littleton's water story this week is really two stories running in parallel. On one front, the Littleton Water Department filed a federal lawsuit seeking to recover costs from building a $20.25 million treatment plant required to remove PFAS contamination from a town drinking water well. The town didn't create the contamination; it simply had to pay to fix it — and now it wants to be made whole. On another front, Littleton is under a Level 3 Critical Drought designation, meaning all nonessential outdoor water use is currently banned. Lawn irrigation, car washing, surface washing — all off limits. The Water Department's routine work of testing 150 monitoring wells and enforcing zoning near drinking water sources takes on added urgency when the aquifer is already under stress. The Board of Health meets September 9th, and drought conditions are likely to be part of the conversation.

Trails and Green Spaces

Not everything this week was infrastructure and litigation. Littleton officially opened the Art Lazarus Trail at Long Lake Park Conservation Area, honoring a volunteer who gave 33 years to local conservation work. The ribbon cutting offered a moment of civic warmth — the kind of occasion that draws neighbors out to celebrate someone who quietly made the town better. The Littleton Conservation Trust's ongoing hiking challenge continues to invite residents to get out on local trails and log their hikes. The Conservation Commission, which held a joint meeting with the Select Board in August and meets again virtually on September 8th, has been active all summer — a reflection of how seriously Littleton takes the green spaces that define its character.

What's Coming

The fall civic calendar fills up quickly. The Zoning Board of Appeals has a public hearing scheduled for September 17th on a property at 22 George Street, and the Affordable Housing Trust, which met September 3rd, will continue its work as housing pressure across the region keeps local affordability in the conversation. Planning Board meetings, a Conservation Commission session, and the Select Board's next regular meeting on September 14th all arrive in the coming days. The season ahead looks as busy as the one just past.
